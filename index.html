🎯 What Is a Panel Discussion?

A panel discussion is when a small group of people sit together in front of an audience and have a focused conversation about a topic. Each person shares their ideas, listens to others, and responds respectfully. Think of it like a roundtable conversation where everyone gets to speak.

Step 2 of 5

Plan — Build Your Panel Discussion

Now that you have your ideas, it’s time to organize them. A good plan means your panel discussion will be clear, focused, and interesting to your audience.

📋 The Parts of a Panel Discussion

Every panel discussion has three main parts: (1) an Introduction that hooks the audience and introduces the topic and panel members, (2) the Main Discussion where each member shares ideas and responds to others, and (3) a Conclusion that wraps up the key points and leaves the audience with something to think about.

🎮Plan Your Introduction

The introduction is the first thing your audience hears. It needs to:

  • Grab the audience’s attention (a question, a surprising fact, or a short example works great)
  • Tell the audience what your topic is
  • Introduce each panel member and what they will focus on

Step 3 of 5

Draft — Write Your Speaking Notes

Now you’ll turn your plan into actual speaking notes. You’re not writing a speech to memorize — you’re writing notes that help you speak confidently and naturally.

📝 Drafting for a Panel Discussion

When you draft for a panel discussion, use formal language — speak like you would in class, not like a text message. Use precise words instead of vague ones. Your notes should be detailed enough that you can speak for 2–3 minutes on your topic.

👥Formal vs. Informal Language

Check out the difference:

❌ Informal (avoid this)

“So like, the woman in the book was going through a lot of stuff and she kind of dealt with it okay I guess.”

✔ Formal (use this)

“The woman in Hidden Figures faced significant discrimination. She responded by demonstrating her exceptional skills and determination every single day.”

Instead of “good”try: effective, powerful, successful, valuable
Instead of “hard”try: challenging, difficult, demanding, burdensome
Instead of “stuff”try: challenges, obstacles, circumstances, situations
Instead of “got through it”try: persevered, overcame, demonstrated resilience
📄Draft Your Speaking Notes

Write your speaking notes for your part of the panel. Include:

  • A clear opening sentence that states your focus area
  • Your 3 main points, each explained in 2–3 sentences
  • At least one text reference (Hidden Figures or Gambian Women sources)
  • A connecting sentence that ties your ideas back to the main prompt

Example Opening Sentences

“My focus today is on how education can be a powerful tool for coping with life’s challenges.”

“I will be discussing how community support helped individuals in both of our texts persevere through difficult situations.”

🤝How to Respond to Panel Members

In a panel discussion, you need to be ready to respond when someone else speaks. Here are phrases to use:

To agree and add on:“I agree with [name]’s point, and I would add that...”
To respectfully disagree:“That’s an interesting perspective. I see it slightly differently because...”
To ask a follow-up:“Can you say more about...?” or “What do you mean by...?”
To make a connection:“What [name] said connects to my point about...”

Step 4 of 5

Revise — Make Your Draft Stronger

Revising means improving the content and ideas in your draft. Ask yourself: “Is this as clear and powerful as it can be?” Every great writer revises — it’s a normal and important part of the process.

🔄 Revising vs. Editing

When you revise, you focus on making your ideas stronger — adding details, rearranging points, cutting things that don’t belong. Editing (Step 5) is different — that’s when you fix grammar and spelling. For now, focus only on your ideas.

🔎Focus Area 1: Expand Your Ideas

Look at your draft. Are any points too short or unclear? A strong panel discussion point has three parts:

  • The main idea stated clearly
  • An example or evidence from one of the texts
  • An explanation of why this matters or connects to the prompt
Before Revision (Too Short)

“Katherine Johnson worked hard and things got better.”

After Revision (Much Stronger)

“Katherine Johnson’s approach to dealing with discrimination was to focus on doing her work with absolute precision. In Hidden Figures, she double-checked NASA’s calculations herself, refusing to let unfair treatment stop her from contributing. This shows that one powerful coping strategy is channeling your energy into doing excellent work, rather than being defeated by obstacles.”

🔗Focus Area 2: Add Transition Words

Transitions connect your ideas and help the audience follow your thinking. Without transitions, your ideas sound like a random list. With transitions, they sound like a real argument.

To add a point:“Furthermore...” / “In addition to this...” / “Another key strategy is...”
To show a result:“As a result...” / “This led to...” / “Because of this...”
To reference a text:“This is evident in...” / “For example, in the text...” / “The sources show that...”
To wrap up a point:“This demonstrates that...” / “Ultimately...” / “In other words...”

Step 5 of 5

Edit & Present — Polish and Perform

This is your final step! Fix any last errors in your writing, then get ready to stand up and present your panel discussion with confidence.

✅ Editing vs. Revising

Remember: Revising (Step 4) is about making your ideas stronger. Editing (this step) is about correctness — fixing spelling, punctuation, grammar, and word choice so your writing is clean and professional.

Editing Checklist
🔎
Clarity — Are your sentences easy to understand?

Read each sentence and ask: “If someone heard this for the first time, would they understand it?”

  • Every sentence has one clear main idea.
  • I haven’t used “it,” “they,” or “this” without being clear about what they refer to.
  • I haven’t written overly long or confusing run-on sentences.
💬
Word Choice — Are your words precise and formal?

Replace vague or informal words with more specific, powerful ones.

Replace “good” with:effective, powerful, successful, valuable
Replace “bad” with:harmful, damaging, unjust, difficult
Replace “things” with:obstacles, challenges, strategies, opportunities
Replace “showed” with:demonstrated, illustrated, revealed, proved
  • I replaced vague words like “stuff,” “things,” “good,” and “bad” with precise words.
  • My language sounds appropriate for a formal class presentation.
⚙️
Mechanics — Spelling, Punctuation & Grammar

Check the technical stuff. Read your work slowly — even read it backward, sentence by sentence, to catch errors.

  • I checked for spelling errors, especially names from the texts.
  • Every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with the right punctuation.
  • I used commas correctly in lists and after transition words.
  • Book titles are italicized or underlined (e.g., Hidden Figures).

Presentation Tips
🎤Get Ready to Present!
📢 Volume & PaceSpeak loudly enough for the whole audience to hear. Slow down when you say something important — don’t rush.
👁️ Eye ContactLook up from your notes and make eye contact with the audience. Glance at your notes, but don’t read every word.
🧍 Body LanguageSit up straight. Keep your hands still or use them to emphasize points. Nod when your panel members speak.
👓 Active ListeningWhen others speak, look at them and really listen. Be ready to respond, agree, or add to what they say.
💡 Pro Tip: Practice Out Loud

The best way to prepare is to say your notes out loud at least once before the real thing. Practice in front of a mirror, with a friend, or just by yourself. Your brain and mouth need to practice together!
